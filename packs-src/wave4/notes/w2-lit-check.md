## Changes (before → after, reason)

- n1 TWIN-RHYME TIME $200: "Magicians' rhyming patter, it probably gave English the shorter word 'hoax'" → "Stage magicians' rhyming patter, it's also the title of a 1993 Bette Midler witch comedy" (hocus-pocus kept). The hoax link is only "probably" in the OED; the movie fact is certain and keeps the row easy.
- n1 GAZETTEER GLOSSARY $1000: "Bayonne ... is the likely source of this blade" / bayonet → "Mosul, an ancient trading city on the Tigris, gave its name to this thin, plain-woven cotton cloth" / muslin. Bayonet from Bayonne is "perhaps" in the dictionaries; muslin (French mousseline, Italian mussolina, from Mosul) is the settled etymology. Magenta was considered but its battle fact is already in our packs.
- n1 LANDLUBBER LINGO $200: "New hands had to know every line ... 'learn' these" / the ropes → "Sheets are the ropes that trim a sail, so a staggering drunk is 'three sheets to' this" / the wind. "Learn the ropes" has a rival theatre origin; "three sheets to the wind" is unambiguously nautical.
- n2 THE SILENT TYPE $1600: "called 'the old man's friend' by Dr. William Osler" → "called 'the friend of the aged' by Dr. William Osler". Osler's printed words (Principles and Practice of Medicine, later editions) are "pneumonia may well be called the friend of the aged"; "the old man's friend" is the popular paraphrase.
- n2 META-PHRASES $800: "An Oxford don's sound swaps, like 'a well-boiled icicle,' gave his name to this kind of slip" → "Sound swaps like 'a well-boiled icicle' are named for an Oxford don, giving us this kind of slip". Most famous spoonerisms, this one included, are apocryphal; the clue no longer says Spooner said it.
- e1 POOR RICHARD SAYS $400: "Ben Franklin's almanac warned" → "Ben Franklin warned the hosts of his day". The old wording gave away the $600 response (an almanac).
- e1 INITIALLY SPEAKING $600: "'Etc.' at the end of a list is short for this Latin phrase" / et cetera → "Carved on many an old tombstone, R.I.P. stands for these three words" / rest in peace. "Etc." is a clipped word, not initials, so it broke the category's gimmick.
- e2 LATIN FOR BEGINNERS $400: "this motto has 13 letters, one per colony" → "this 13-letter Latin motto also rides on U.S. coins". That the 13 letters stand for the colonies is a later reading, not a documented intent; the "out of many, one" version I tried first was flagged as too close to a televised clue. The motto has been required on U.S. coins since the Coinage Act of 1873.

## The writer's doubts, resolved

- Hoax, bayonet, "the ropes": replaced (above).
- Namby-pamby: Henry Carey's 1725 "Namby Pamby" mocking Ambrose Philips is the OED's origin. Kept.
- Canter from "Canterbury pace/gallop": the standard dictionary etymology, not hedged. Kept.
- "Taken aback": the nautical sense (sails laid back against the mast) is the attested first use. Kept.
- William Carlos Williams writing between patients: well documented, and the clue avoids the prescription-pad legend. Kept.
- Qantas: founded 16 November 1920 in Queensland (registered in Brisbane, first base at Winton). Kept.
- "Gnomes of Zurich": Harold Wilson (1956) and George Brown (1964) were both Labour, so "British Labour politicians" is safe. Kept.
- Osler: wording corrected (above).
- 'Frankenstein' as epistolary: Walton's letters frame the whole novel, and it is routinely called epistolary. Kept.
- Faulkner's 'A Fable' outline on the Rowan Oak office walls: still there today. Kept.
- 'A Visit from St. Nicholas': the 1823 Troy Sentinel printing had "Dunder and Blixem", but Moore's own 1844 text reads "Donder and Blitzen", which the clue quotes. "Blitzen" is correct for the clue as written. Kept.
- Gingerbread house: the Grimms say bread, cake and sugar, so "often said" is accurate. Kept.
